A drinking glass that performs. *Sun Dial* takes its name and its logic from the sundial: an instrument that makes time visible not by displaying it, but by translating the position of light into a readable pattern on a surface. The glasses do the same — their engraved walls catch, fragment, and redirect light as the angle of viewing and the level of liquid constantly shift.

<h2>Light refraction</h2>

The project began with an exploration of the relationship between form, light, and interaction. The starting point was the study of light refraction — not as a decorative effect applied to glass, but as the generative principle behind the form itself.

The process moved from a simple prism, simulating increasingly complex patterns, until surfaces emerged that could fragment, reflect, and modulate light in unexpected ways. Each subsequent variation pushed the engraving logic further, producing effects that change continuously as the user handles the glass.

<h2>Form and function</h2>

The inspiration for the engraving distribution came from solar projection maps: in planisphere representations, light gradients spread across conical surfaces, marking the passage between the day zone and the night zone with a visual drama that changes with the observer's position.

This principle was translated directly into the whisky glass. The cylinder becomes a surface capable of modulating light in the same suggestive way, turning the act of drinking into an evolving play of reflections and shadows.

<h2>The pattern system</h2>

From a small set of primitive elements, a generative engraving system was developed that can produce an entire family of products from a single coherent language. Four base marks — the infinite line, the long line, the rectangle, and the short line — combine into patterns that follow or are bounded by a guiding curve. The position of each element within its row can vary randomly within a defined range, meaning the system could hypothetically generate an unlimited series of unique combinations from an algorithm.

<h2>Y2K Futurism</h2>

To build a product family with a broader and more autonomous identity, the research moved beyond the solar map concept and pushed toward more expressive formal languages. The reference point was Y2K Futurism — the aesthetic current born between the late 1990s and early 2000s that fused optimistic visions of the future with fluid forms, iridescent colours, and pop references, mixing glamour and space with a hyper-stylistic sensibility.

This reference was interpreted radically but within the constraints of what is actually producible in crystal. Increasingly complex geometries were tested, and the base structure of the glass was varied up to the limit of manufacturing feasibility — generating new light refractions and giving formal identity to an entirely new design language for the collection.

<h2>The complete collection</h2>

The collection is articulated across six pieces in two macro-categories: a more traditional group tied to recognisable and stable forms, and a more experimental group defined by structural variations that amplify the visual and perceptual effect. All six share the same engraving system and the same day/night surface logic.
